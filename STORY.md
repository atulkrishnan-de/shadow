# SHADOW

*He is not haunting the building. The building is still using him.*

---

## The phenomenon

Something is wrong with a few minutes inside that building, and nobody has ever known why.

It was not made. It was **found** — first written down in a 1961 site survey as *anomalous
interval recorded, sub-level 2*, forty years before anyone thought of doing anything with
it. A stretch of time down there does not close. It runs, and reaches its end, and is at its
beginning again, the way a scratched record is not broken, only stuck.

The plant was already standing over it. Nothing about the location was chosen; it is simply
where the thing is. For four decades it was a line in a filing cabinet, an oddity the night
shift told each other about, a reason certain instruments in sub-level 2 read wrong.

Nobody ever explained it. **That is the point of it** — the game never explains it either.
There is no scientist with a theory, no whiteboard, no cause. There is a hole in the
afternoon and a company that worked out what it was worth.

---

## The idea

A loop is free labour.

That is what they finally did with it, and it is what makes the rest of the story go. If you
can hold a man inside a repeating interval, you do not get one man for four minutes. You get
four minutes of work, then four minutes of work again, and again, out of the same wage and
the same body. Put it on a spreadsheet and it is the most profitable thing anyone at Sector C
ever found in a filing cabinet.

The programme was called **temporal containment** — containment, because you contain a
phenomenon, you do not contain something you invented. In the funding documents: *interval
stabilisation for hazardous-environment work.* Hold the anomaly steady, put an operator
inside it, let him repeat the same dangerous four minutes as many times as the task needs.

The plant was closing. The programme was the only part of the site still hiring.

---

## The man

A maintenance fitter. Twelve years on the floor before any of this, which is why his hands
know where the isolator boxes are, and why he walks into a room already knowing which
corridor carries the weight of the one above it.

When the fitting work dried up, the programme was taking volunteers from the existing
workforce — people already cleared for site, already used to the machines, and, unspoken,
people whose absence would be an internal matter rather than a news story.

**He signed.** Not heroically. The way anyone signs: the money was the money that was
available, there was a child, and the alternative was no job in a town with one employer.

That signature is the only thing he is guilty of, and the game never lets him off it. He is
not a man who did something terrible. He is a man who agreed to something, on a form, for
money, and then found out what he had agreed to.

---

## What the tests actually were

Not measurement. Rehearsal.

They needed to know whether a looped operator could work *with his own earlier iterations* —
whether instance four could stand on a plate instance one had learned to reach, whether five
of him could run a task that needed five men. A workforce of one.

So the tests were tasks. Hold this down. Cross when the press lifts. Ride the lift, throw the
switch, be at the door in time. Exactly the puzzles in the game, because they **are** the
puzzles in the game.

> Every door in this building asks for someone who is not here.

Which means something uncomfortable. When the player coordinates four Shadows to open a
door, the player completes the experiment. It worked. The hypothesis was correct. You are
the proof.

---

## 04/11 — the test that did not close

The interlock on Hydraulic Press 3 was disabled that morning, because a guard is for
protecting an operator you intend to get back, and the whole point of the trial was that the
interval resets whatever happens inside it.

That day's test was the first attempt to *close* the loop — to end the interval and bring
him out of it.

It did not close. The field locked open with him inside.

Nobody was killed, which is why there is no memorial and no inquiry anyone remembers. There
was an incident, an evacuation, an insurance position, and a site that became too expensive
to certify and too awkward to demolish. The gates were chained. Everyone went home.

His watch stopped at 09:41 and never started again.

---

## How he gets back — the phenomenon takes him

The release on 04/11 did not put him back on the shelf. It half-worked, which was worse than
failing: it got him out of the room, and left him **unfastened from his own timeline.**

He lives outside. He goes home, he ages, fourteen years of them. And then, without warning
and without choosing it, he is somewhere else: standing at the maintenance intake of a
condemned plant at 09:41 on a Saturday in November, with the lights on and the air wrong and
a slight, unplaceable memory of the corridor in front of him.

The anomaly reaches for what it has already had hold of. It has reached for him fourteen
hundred times — the counter on the observation console reads `1,4—` before the digits give
out.

**But it opens the door. It does not walk him through it.**

Every one of those times he has stood at that threshold, with the way back to the car park
directly behind him, and gone in. That distinction is the whole game. He is not dragged and
he is not trapped. He is summoned, repeatedly, by something that does not want anything —
and he answers.

---

## What the loop takes

Each pass costs him something. Not memory in a clean, cinematic way — more the erosion of a
person who has done nothing else for a very long time. He knows the building the way you know
your own staircase in the dark, and he could not tell you the name of the street it is on.

> You know which corridors are load-bearing.
> *You have never been inside this building.*

He walked in that morning with his pockets full: a watch, a photograph, house keys, a letter
he had been meaning to post, a drawing his daughter did at the kitchen table. The field has
been stripping those off him one cycle at a time and leaving them where they fell.

---

## The keepsakes — the pieces of him the building kept

Scattered through the facility, off the route and up on ledges, are his own things.

> Your watch.
> *It stopped at 09:41 and never started again.*

> A drawing, in crayon.
> *Two figures. One of them is much taller.*

Picking one up puts a piece of him back, and a slightly more complete man holds on a little
longer before the field lets go and the shift starts again. That is the +5 seconds: not a
power-up, an act of self-assembly.

They escalate — *time*, then *a face*, then *a home*, then *words*, then *the child* — and by
the last one, picking it up costs more than it gives.

And a Shadow can never collect one. Only he can. **Your past selves can hold a door open for
you; they cannot put you back together.**

---

## The Shadows — what the purple figures are

Not ghosts. Not metaphors. **Product.**

Every iteration the field kept is another unit of the workforce they were building, still
running its task, with no knowledge that anyone is watching. Their meaning changes three
times and the game never comments on any of it:

| | |
|---|---|
| **At first — a tool** | Something that holds a plate down so a door stays open. Useful. Impersonal. Exactly how the programme regarded him. |
| **Then — a cost** | To get past the press he must send one of them under the hammer on purpose. He does to himself what was done to him. |
| **At the end — a count** | Every version of himself it took, standing on one floor. The experiment's yield, and the only reason he gets out. |

The sorting bay is worse than it looks. A shelf of personal effects, catalogued and
unclaimed — and he is not the first name in that ledger.

> SORTING BAY 2 — personal effects, unclaimed

---

## The observation deck

Forty feet up, behind green glass, with a clear view of the whole floor.

He has been looked at from that room several thousand times. He has never been in it. When
the player finally rides the lift and stands there, it is the first time in fourteen years he
is on the side of the glass that does the watching.

> This is where they watched from.
> *You have only ever seen it from the other side.*

There is a desk, a dead monitor, a photograph somebody left behind in a hurry. Whoever sat
here went home that afternoon and has had fourteen more years of life since.

---

## The turn — it opens the door, it does not walk him through

The obvious version of this story is that he is trapped. He is not, and the game's whole
argument depends on him not being.

The anomaly summons. That part is not his fault and never was — he did not ask to be
unfastened, and he cannot predict when it takes him. But it can only put him on the
threshold. It cannot cross it for him, and the containment only re-engages once a subject
steps onto the first plate.

> The field has held for fourteen years.
> *It has only ever needed the one subject.*

He has stood at that intake door fourteen hundred times, with the access road and the weeds
coming up through the concrete directly behind him, and gone in. Every single time.

Because in there it is still that Saturday morning, and he is still the man he was at 09:41,
and nothing has been taken from him yet.

Out here is the other thing. Out here he did not vanish — that would have been cleaner.
He came home. He has been coming home for fourteen years as a man who keeps going away
without warning and cannot say where, and who is never entirely in the room when he is in
it. Somewhere out here is a young woman who drew two figures at a kitchen table and grew all
the way up with a father who was almost always elsewhere.

**He is not going back because he cannot stay away. He is going back because it is the one
place where none of that has happened to him yet.**

---

## The theme — what the game is actually saying

Two things, and neither is about forgiveness.

**One.** You can use your past without being able to fix it. The loop is *stable*: earlier
versions of you can be commanded, coordinated, relied upon — never corrected. Nothing you do
this time changes what they did. That constraint is the thesis, and it is enforced in code
before it is ever spoken.

**Two.** Some harm has no villain left to confront. There is no scientist to find, no
confession to extract, no lever that undoes it. The people who did this filed the incident
and got on with their lives. The only thing still in his control is whether he clocks on
again.

The ending offers no absolution and no reunion. It offers a man walking out of a door into a
year he has not lived in — the smallest possible victory, and a real one.

---

## What the player is never told

None of the above is stated in the game. There is no word *subject*, no consent form, no
scientist, no explanation of the programme, and no confirmation of who made the drawing.

What they get: a badge with the wrong date, a shelf of unclaimed effects, a bypass
authorisation painted on a wall, a pack already lying in the pit, a watch stopped at 09:41, a
drawing of two figures, an observation deck they finally stand inside, and a counter with its
digits burned out.

This document exists so that every object in that building was placed by someone who knew.

---

## How the fiction became the rules

| Fiction | Mechanic |
|---|---|
| The interval has a fixed length. At the end the field resets it. | the countdown timer |
| The field keeps the iteration that failed; it goes on running its task. | Shadow record and replay |
| The loop is stable — the past can be used, never overwritten. | deterministic replay, no AI |
| The programme's product is a workforce made of one man. | multiple simultaneous Shadows |
| Every instance kept is more strain on a containment that already failed. | each Shadow burns out a light, raises the klaxon |
| The field strips a little of him each cycle and leaves it where it falls. | keepsakes: +5s, player-only |

The room going dark is not atmosphere. It is the machine failing under the weight of how many
of him are in it. And because the Shadows glow, the light his iterations burn out is replaced
by the light his iterations cast.

---

## The reveal, in order played

**I · INTAKE** — Mundane. A maintenance sign. The only wrong note: he knows a building he has
never entered, and nothing in it has decayed the way fourteen years should have.

**II · SORTING** — A shelf of effects nobody claimed. A badge in the third tray: his
photograph, his name, *a date that is not this one.*

**III · PRESS ROOM** — His own pack is already lying in the water at the bottom of the pit. He
is still wearing his.

**IV · OBSERVATION** — The room he has been watched from. The counter with its digits burned
out. And the understanding that the door was never the puzzle.
